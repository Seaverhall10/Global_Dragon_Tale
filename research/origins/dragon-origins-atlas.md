# Where the Dragon Begins: Evidence Atlas I
## Direct finding
The record does **not** currently support a single “first dragon.” It supports a sequence of increasingly diagnostic evidence:

1. A deeply engraved **serpentine motif** at Abri Pataud, France, in a Level 3 context modeled to about **28,420–26,910 cal BP**. The excavators described curving lines and punctuations—not an anatomically identified snake—so this is the **earliest candidate**, not the first secure serpent.[^1][^2][^3]
2. A dense, unmistakably intentional **snake-image tradition** in early sedentary Southwest Asia, especially Tell Qaramel, Körtiktepe, Jerf el Ahmar, and Göbekli Tepe, during roughly the **11th–9th millennia BCE**.[^4][^5][^6][^7]
3. **Composite serpent-feline monsters** in Late Uruk imagery around **3500–3100 BCE**. These are the earliest objects in this sequence that comfortably satisfy a morphological definition of “dragon-like”: imaginary animals deliberately combining dangerous species.[^8][^9]
4. A securely attested written dragon word, Sumerian **ušumgal**, in sources beginning in Early Dynastic IIIb and continuing through later periods; the lexical corpus translates it as “dragon.”[^10]
5. A named divine context on Gudea’s libation vessel from Girsu, dated **2120–2110 BCE**, where paired serpents and hybrid serpent-dragons accompany a dedication to Ningishzida.[^11]

This evidence requires two parallel chronologies: **serpent imagery** begins much earlier than **dragons properly defined**.
## Definitions
| Category | Operational test | What it permits |
|---|---|---|
| Serpentiform | Wavy or curvilinear form, but no decisive anatomy | Candidate only; never proof of a snake or myth |
| Represented snake | Diagnostic body/head treatment or repeated contextual agreement among specialists | Secure serpent imagery |
| Symbolic serpent | Repetition, conspicuous placement, patterned combinations, or special depositional context | Symbolic importance is plausible; exact meaning remains unknown |
| Dragon-like hybrid | Impossible composite animal incorporating serpentine and other animal features | “Dragon-like” is defensible even without a native name |
| Named dragon | Written source applies a word translated by specialists as dragon/great serpent | Historical dragon tradition |

The categories must not be collapsed. A snake image is not automatically a deity, an image in a communal building is not automatically religious, and a serpentiform line is not automatically a snake.

### Snake reliefs
Open-access archaeological publication plates show snakes on several Göbekli Tepe pillars. In the first four excavated enclosures, snakes were reported as 28.4% of animal representations—approximately twice the fox share—while grouped snakes were conservatively counted as one occurrence; this establishes prominence, not a translation of what the snakes “meant.”[^25]

The excavators’ project blog independently describes snakes as among the most frequent animals, occurring on architecture and portable objects; Building A includes a “net of snakes,” and a similar miniature plaque suggests deliberate repetition across media.[^7][^22]
### Abri Pataud’s problem
The Abri Pataud Level 3 publication says that a large fallen limestone block used as the front wall of the Lens 2 “long-house” carries a deeply engraved **“serpentine” motif of curvilinear lines and punctuations**. The wording is morphological: it does not call the design a serpent, animal, deity, or narrative figure.[^1]

Level 3 is dated around 24,000 radiocarbon years BP, with a modeled calibrated interval of 30,030–27,350 cal BP in one recent study and 28,420–26,910 cal BP in a published stratigraphic synthesis. The discrepancy reflects chronology/model choices, so the project should publish a range and its source rather than a false single date.[^2][^3]

**Verdict:** Abri Pataud is presently the earliest documented item in this investigation that can be placed in a controlled archaeological context and explicitly described by its publishers as “serpentine.” It is **not** yet the earliest proven snake picture. The original Movius plate 33 and the block itself must be inspected before making any anatomical claim.[^1]
## Early Neolithic corpus
Körtiktepe provides the long-duration background. Its settlement sequence is reported from approximately 10,700 to 9300 BCE, and its decorated vessels and plaques repeatedly carry snakes/vipers alongside scorpions, birds, wild mammals, and composite beings.[^5][^23]

Tell Qaramel may start earlier as a settlement, with site horizons extending from 10,890 BCE, and PPNA ground-stone objects carry zoomorphic motifs identified as snakes and birds. However, the present evidence does not yet demonstrate that the earliest dated Qaramel horizon contains each specific snake object; object-level stratigraphic assignments remain necessary.[^4][^20]

At Jerf el Ahmar, multiple publications report snakes on small plaques, sometimes combined with a bird, fox, and geometric motifs. This combination can reasonably be called compositional imagery, but describing it as a written myth would exceed the evidence.[^6][^26]

At Göbekli Tepe, frequency, monumental placement, clustering, and duplication on portable items make random doodling implausible. Proposed meanings include death, threat, phallic symbolism, shamanism, narrative identity, and evolved fear responses; the images alone do not decide among these hypotheses.[^27][^25]
## From snake to dragon
The transition is not a straight evolutionary ladder. Thousands of years separate the early Neolithic snake corpora from urban Mesopotamian hybrid monsters, and there is no demonstrated chain of direct transmission across every intervening millennium.

Late Uruk cylinder seals depict **serpo-felines**: lion bodies and heads joined to long, twisting serpent necks. The Morgan Library dates one ritual-labor seal to approximately 3500–3100 BCE and interprets the composites as stronger than real-world animals and as guardians framing temple-associated labor.[^8]

Calling these creatures “dragons” remains a modern classificatory choice, but **dragon-like hybrid** is evidentially sound: they are deliberately impossible composites whose serpentine and feline traits amplify danger or power.[^9][^8]

The written record later supplies native terminology. ORACC’s Electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary records **ušumgal** as “dragon,” with attestations beginning in Early Dynastic IIIb and continuing through Lagash II, Ur III, Old Babylonian, and later periods.[^10]

The Gudea vessel then joins inscription, deity, serpents, and hybrids in one securely dated artifact. The Louvre dates it to 2120–2110 BCE, identifies the decoration as Ningishzida and serpent, records its Sumerian dedication, and gives its discovery place as Girsu/Tello.[^11]
## Claim audit
| Claim | Status | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| “The first serpent appears at Abri Pataud.” | **Too strong** | The publication says “serpentine motif,” not animal.[^1] |
| “The earliest secure serpent tradition is Neolithic Southwest Asia.” | **Supported** | Multiple sites, media, repeated motifs, and diagnostic heads/bodies produce a robust regional corpus.[^5][^6][^7] |
| “Göbekli Tepe contains the first snake image.” | **Unsupported** | Candidate imagery is older; several Southwest Asian sites overlap chronologically.[^1][^5] |
| “Göbekli Tepe proves a snake religion.” | **Unsupported** | Frequency and placement prove importance, not a specific theology.[^25][^7] |
| “Late Uruk serpo-felines are early dragons.” | **Defensible with qualification** | They are secure fantastic serpent-feline hybrids; “dragon” is a comparative modern category.[^8][^9] |
| “ušumgal is an early written dragon term.” | **Supported** | The specialist lexical corpus translates it as dragon and supplies its attestation periods.[^10] |
| “Tiamat begins the dragon tradition.” | **False framing** | Serpentiform, snake, hybrid, and written-dragon evidence all precede the mature Tiamat narrative sequence established in the earlier project phase.[^10] |
## Research standard
Every future entry should contain:

- **Object identity:** museum/accession number where one exists.
- **Find context:** trench, locus, layer, building, burial, or secondary provenance.
- **Dating chain:** direct date, associated date, typological date, or historical reign—clearly distinguished.
- **Image rights:** photographer, repository, license, and stable source page.
- **Description before interpretation:** observable form first; proposed meaning second.
- **Confidence grade:** A (secure), B (probable), C (candidate), D (rejected/obsolete).
- **Competing readings:** at least one serious alternative where interpretation is disputed.
- **Transmission status:** demonstrated contact, plausible contact, independent recurrence, or unknown.
## Next evidence tasks
1. Obtain Movius 1977, plate 33, or a museum photograph of the Abri Pataud engraved block; the entire Paleolithic claim depends on seeing the object rather than repeating “serpentine.”[^1]
2. Audit El Pendo against the museum catalogue and original figure; it must remain excluded from the main chronology until the object and layer are verified.
3. Build an object-level catalogue for Tell Qaramel, Körtiktepe, Jerf el Ahmar, and Göbekli Tepe, including figure numbers and exact contexts.
4. Separate ordinary snakes from composite creatures, and separate both from native lexical labels.
5. Continue the chronology through Early Dynastic Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus sphere, and early China without assuming diffusion unless contact evidence exists.
## Working conclusion
The most convincing formulation is: **the first candidate serpentine design in the present corpus is the Gravettian block from Abri Pataud, around 28,000–27,000 cal BP; the first convincing large-scale serpent-image tradition appears in early Neolithic Southwest Asia by the 11th–10th millennia BCE; and the first strong dragon-like hybrids appear in Late Uruk Mesopotamia by approximately 3500–3100 BCE.** A native written dragon term is firmly documented later in Sumerian.[^1][^5][^7][^10][^8]
